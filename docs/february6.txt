On February 6, 2023, at 4:17 AM local time, a 7.8 magnitude earthquake struck southeastern Turkey near the city of Kahramanmaras. A second major earthquake of 7.7 magnitude followed hours later. The disaster affected 11 provinces and killed more than 50,000 people, making it one of the deadliest earthquakes in Turkish history.

Survivors described waking to the sound of collapsing buildings. Many lost their entire families in seconds. Entire neighborhoods that had stood for generations were reduced to rubble in moments. People who survived spoke of standing outside in the cold darkness, calling names into the ruins, hearing no response.

One survivor from Hatay said: "I knocked on every door that remained standing, looking for my children. Some doors opened. Most did not." This image — knocking on doors that no longer have walls around them — became a symbol of the disaster's devastation.

The earthquake was not just a natural disaster. It exposed decades of poor construction, corruption, and neglect. Young people who survived spoke of a loss of trust — in institutions, in the future, in the idea that the ground beneath them was solid. For many, February 6 became a threshold: life before, and life after. A door that opened without their consent, into a world they did not choose.
The earthquake also raised questions about generational trauma and collective grief. In Turkish culture, the concept of "kapı" — door — carries deep resonance. The door of a home is not just an entrance; it is the boundary between safety and the outside world, between the self and everything else. When those doors collapsed on February 6, something more than architecture was destroyed.

Young survivors, many of them university students, described a particular kind of grief: mourning not just people, but futures. Plans that would never happen. Cities that would not be rebuilt the same way. The earthquake became a generational marker, the way September 11 was for Americans or the Vietnam War for an earlier generation — a before and after that lodged itself permanently in identity.

What connects February 6 to the spirit of Dylan's song is not tragedy itself, but the human response to threshold. The song does not despair. It asks. "Knock, knock, knockin' on heaven's door." There is still agency in the knocking. Even at the limit, even in loss, there is still a hand raised, still a question posed. That is what the survivors of February 6 carried with them — the act of knocking, even when they did not know what was on the other side.
