In January 1973, the Paris Peace Accords were signed, officially ending direct United States military involvement in the Vietnam War. For the soldiers who returned home, the end of the war was not a celebration — it was a threshold into a world that had moved on without them.

Vietnam veterans came back to a country deeply divided. Unlike soldiers from previous wars, they were not welcomed as heroes. Many faced silence, resentment, or outright hostility. The counterculture movement that had grown during the war years had turned against the conflict, and those who had fought in it often carried that rejection with them for the rest of their lives.

The psychological toll was immense. Post-traumatic stress disorder, though not yet named, was widespread. Veterans described feeling like strangers in their own homes, unable to explain what they had seen or translate their experience into the language of ordinary life. Many said the hardest door to knock on was the door to their own past — the person they had been before the war.

1973 was also the year of the final withdrawal of American troops from Vietnam. For many soldiers it was not an ending but a suspension — a war that would continue inside them long after the last helicopter left. The Paris Accords did not bring peace to those who had lived it. It brought a kind of silence that was harder to bear than the noise.

Bob Dylan's generation had grown up with the war as the defining political and moral crisis of their era. The draft, the protests, the Kent State shootings in 1970, the Pentagon Papers in 1971 — these events shaped a collective wound. When Dylan wrote "Knockin' on Heaven's Door" in 1973, the shadow of Vietnam hung over everything. The dying sheriff in Pat Garrett and Billy the Kid carried echoes of every young man who had been sent somewhere he did not choose and asked to carry something too heavy to bring home.
