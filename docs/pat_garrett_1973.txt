Sam Peckinpah's "Pat Garrett & Billy the Kid" (1973) is the film that gave birth to "Knockin' on Heaven's Door." It is a Western, but it is also an elegy — for the frontier, for friendship, for a certain kind of American myth that was dying as surely as Billy the Kid himself.

The story is simple on the surface. Pat Garrett, once an outlaw who rode with Billy the Kid, has become a lawman. He is hired by the Santa Fe Ring — the establishment, the money — to hunt down and kill his old friend Billy. The film follows both men as they move toward their inevitable meeting: one running, one pursuing, neither fully believing in what he is doing.

Peckinpah made the film in 1973, the same year the Vietnam War was entering its final chapter. The parallels were not lost on him. Here was a story about men sent to do the establishment's dirty work, killing people they might once have called brothers. Here was a story about what happens when the frontier closes — when there is no more space to run to, no more wilderness to disappear into. The West that Billy the Kid represented was over. The counterculture's dream of that same open space — freedom, escape, living outside the system — was also ending.

The dying sheriff scene, where "Knockin' on Heaven's Door" first plays, is one of the great moments in film music. The sheriff, played by Slim Pickens, has been shot. He walks to the river and sits at its edge, watching the water. His wife comes and sits beside him. They say nothing. The sun sets. Dylan's song begins. It is the sound of someone arriving at a door they cannot refuse.

Dylan himself appears in the film as "Alias," a mysterious figure who drifts through the story without a clear function. He is an observer, a ghost — present but not quite part of the world he is witnessing. This is how Dylan moved through his own era: always slightly outside it, watching, turning what he saw into music.

Billy the Kid, in the mythology of the American West, was a figure of impossible freedom. He was also nineteen years old when he was killed. The film does not romanticize him — it mourns him, and with him mourns the idea that individuals can outrun the systems that pursue them. By 1973, that idea had been tested by a decade of counterculture, Vietnam, civil rights, Watergate, and assassinations. The verdict was complicated. But the longing remained.

"Knockin' on Heaven's Door" asks no political questions. It makes no argument. It simply places a person at a threshold — the end of something, the beginning of nothing certain — and holds the moment. That is why it has outlasted its original context. Every generation finds itself standing at some version of that river bank, watching the sun go down on something they cannot hold.
